The one idea underneath all of this. OLS residuals work because they have a common scale, constant variance, and a known reference distribution. Non-identity links, mean-variance coupling, and discreteness destroy those properties one at a time. Randomized quantile residuals — u ~ Uniform(F(y−1), F(y)), then Φ⁻¹(u) — restore all three at once. That is why every path above ends at the same place.

Why 1 before 3

Overdispersion narrows DHARMa's simulation envelope, so ordinary points get flagged as outliers. HW6 flagged a pile of them — it was one variance-function problem in disguise. Fix the family before you interrogate individuals.

Why 3 before 4

An influential point pulls β̂, which bends the residual curve and looks like nonlinearity. Ruling out the local explanation is cheap (drop two rows, refit). The reverse isn't — adding x² for one leverage point gives you a term you can't defend.

Why 3 ⇆ 4 loops

Nonlinearity also produces same-signed residual clusters that look like outliers. Tell: many flags, one direction, one end of X's range → nonlinearity. Few flags, mixed directions → real extreme cases. Expect two passes. That isn't p-hacking.

4Assumptions no residual will ever test

Proportional odds — ordinal models

One β shared across all J−1 cumulative logits. Violated → β̂ is a weighted average hiding where in the outcome scale the effect lives.

See it: plot GOL threshold-specific coefficients vs. the PO estimate. Cluster = holds; fan = violated.

Test: ordinal::nominal_test() (LR) and brant::brant() (Wald). When they disagree, believe the LR test — Brant inherits Hauck-Donner.

Fix: some → PPO · most → GOL · ordering suspect → SLM (are φⱼ monotone in 0–1?) · ordering wrong → MNL.

IIA — multinomial logit

Odds of j over k don't depend on any other alternative. Red bus / blue bus: near-perfect substitutes get treated as independent options.

Test cautiously: Hausman-McFadden and Small-Hsiao both have low power, poor size, and HM can return a negative χ². The restricted model drops a non-random subsample.

Treat IIA as theoretical. Are your categories plausible substitutes? If yes, plan for nested / mixed / conditional logit regardless of the test.

Bonus: SLM φⱼ also diagnose whether your "nominal" outcome secretly has an ordering.

Heterogeneity bias — mixed models

A level-1 predictor correlated with the group random effect contaminates the within-group effect with between-group composition.

Mundlak: add the group mean of X as a level-2 covariate alongside the group-centered X. If the group-mean coefficient is significant and differs from the within coefficient, the original model was conflating two processes.

Is a mixed model warranted at all?

ICC = τ₀₀/(τ₀₀+σ²). For logit, σ² is fixed at π²/3 ≈ 3.29, not estimated. performance::icc() handles this.

>.05 meaningful, >.10 strong — but if clustering is theoretically real, include it anyway. An unneeded RE costs a little efficiency; ignoring real clustering biases every SE.

Random slopes: theory first, then anova(ri, rs, refit=FALSE). Don't test slopes on everything.

5Five ways to get this wrong

MistakeInstead
QQ-plotting deviance/Pearson residuals for a binary or count modelThey're non-normal even under a perfect model. Use quantile residuals. (Still fine for flagging individual outliers.)
Comparing var(y)/mean(y) to 1 as a dispersion testVar(Y) = E[μ] + Var(μ). Covariate-driven variation inflates the ratio. Condition on the model: check_overdispersion().
Leading with p-values from diagnostic testsPlots are the primary evidence. Large N: tests reject on trivia — believe the plot. Small N: tests miss real problems — believe the plot.
Treating check_model() as the endpointIt's a first pass that tells you where to dig. Plot residuals against your own predictors and think about the shapes.
Deleting an inconvenient outlierDelete only for a documented data error, and say so. Otherwise report with and without.
